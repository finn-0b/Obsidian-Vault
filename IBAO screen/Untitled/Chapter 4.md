4.1 INTRODUCTION

In this chapter, we’ll review what it means to sell insurance to the general public. Compared to many other industries that operate on a transactional basis, there are more elements to insurance that need to be considered, including what to do when something goes wrong. This chapter will help you identify what obligations a broker has to the public and their clients.

4.2 BROKER DUTY

In an insurance transaction, brokers are the liaisons between two parties: the public (their client) and the insurer (also their client). Because brokers help the public learn about the insurance they need, identify risks they are exposed to, and establish the conditions of the policy, it’s easy to become confused as to whom they are really representing. Fundamentally, brokers owe a duty to both their clients and the insurance companies they represent. Below are some guidelines to keep the boundaries clear for each group.

4.2.1 Duty to Clients

In Chapter 2: How Insurance Is Organized in Canada, we learned that a broker’s primary duty is to ensure clients receive appropriate insurance advice and coverage. Brokers represent choice to the general public, as the brokerage is not bound to just one insurer in the policies or products it can sell. Additionally, the Registered Insurance Broker Act (RIB Act), which we’ll examine further in Chapter 5: Regulation of Insurance Contracts and Chapter 6: The Regulation of Insurance Brokers, specifically states a broker owes a duty to their clients to be competent, conscientious, diligent, honest, and ethical when performing services on behalf of their clients.

4.2.1.1 Errors and Omissions Claims

Brokers do make mistakes, and some of these can result in financial loss to their clients. The courts view insurance brokers as professionals and, as such, they impose upon brokers a high standard of duty of care. Making an error on a policy or omitting to place the coverage requested by a client can lead to a claim of negligence by the policyholder. In fact, inadequate coverage is the most common cause of errors and omissions (E&O) claims, accounting for more than 50% of all E&O claims. These claims are the result of:

• Failure to provide proper coverages.

• Failure to advise clients of policy exclusions and terms that could affect coverage.

• Failure to place coverage, or placing it too late.

• Making mistakes in coverage.

• Not correctly advising as to the availability of coverage.

Other sources of E&O claims include:

• Misrepresentation and errors in the description of the risk being insured.

• Cancellation and renewal errors.

• Policy change errors.

• Processing delays.

• Binding authority violations (for example, exceeding binding authority).

By RIBO legislation, insurance brokerages in Ontario must purchase professional E&O liability coverage. This requirement helps protect consumers against financial harm if brokers fail to perform their duties. An E&O policy is similar to malpractice insurance purchased by doctors and other health professionals. E&O insurance policies cover all persons working in the brokerage (employees are not required to purchase their own policy).

4.2.1.1.1 Minimizing E&O CLAIMS

To minimize the chance of an E&O claim, brokerage management must develop and publish a set of standardized workflow procedures for each transaction. It is critical that brokerages train all new employees on brokerage procedures and re-train all employees when new or amended procedures are introduced.

The best defense against an E&O claim is a well documented file. This means documenting all client conversations, both written and oral. For example, if a client declines an optional coverage or declines a higher limit of insurance than the broker has recommended, the broker must document this in the client’s file. It is also advisable to send a letter to the client confirming the client’s decision.

4.2.2 Duty to Insurance Companies They Represent

Individual brokers place their clients’ insurance coverages under a contractual agreement with one of the insurers represented at their brokerage. Under this legal agreement, brokers owe a duty to the insurers to tell the truth about the risks they submit to the company underwriters and to not withhold important information.

At first glance, it might seem these two sets of duties are at odds with one another. In fact, they complement each other. Let’s look at an example:

Ben is a long-term client with an excellent driving record. He calls his broker because he has purchased a car for his 17-year-old daughter, Mia, to drive to school each day. Ben will remain the owner of the vehicle. Due to Mia’s age and driving experience, the insurance premium the broker calculates is substantially higher than Ben expected. He asks the broker to find a lower premium. Assuming this is the lowest premium offered by all the insurers the broker represents, what should the broker do?

On the one hand, the broker owes a duty to Ben. The broker might feel an obligation to lie to the insurance company on behalf of Ben by stating he is the principal vehicle operator, not Mia. But on the other hand, doing so is not fair to the insurance company, which would not receive the correct premium for taking on the exposure of insuring the less experienced driver. Additionally, if Mia gets involved in a car accident, or if the car were stolen while under her care, the insurance company would have the right to deny the claim.

Lying on behalf of the client serves neither parties’ interest in this transaction. Additionally, the broker will most likely lose their job and have their license suspended by Registered Insurance Brokers of Ontario (RIBO).

A broker may be tempted to avoid the truth or conceal negative information from an insurer. Perhaps the broker wants to help a friend or family member, or they may not want to lose the client’s business to a competitor. But always remember: not only are such actions illegal, but they serve no one’s interest. Such lack of integrity is never justified.

4.2.2.1 Broker as First-Line Underwriter

In Chapter 2: How Insurance Is Organized in Canada, we learned that the role of insurance company underwriters is to analyze application forms submitted by brokers to ensure applicants’ risks meet the insurer’s established eligibility criteria.

Brokers are often referred to as “first line underwriters” as they are the ones who actually speak with the client. This means they are the first to have the opportunity to assess a client’s risk and determine whether it meets the insurer’s eligibility requirements. The following example illustrates the broker’s role as first-line underwriter:

Broker Angie receives a call from a prospective client inquiring about insurance for his business, Giorgio’s Roofing. Since Angie recently attended an IBAO webinar on the hazards of certain types of roofing work, she asks Giorgio several questions. She discovers his company specializes in hot-tar roofing for flat-roof office and condominium buildings.

Angie reviews the eligibility requirements for roofers as provided by the insurance companies her brokerage represents. In doing so, she learns most of these insurers decline to insure hot-tar roofers and, for those who do, a risk-specific questionnaire must be completed and submitted to an underwriter before the insurer will consider quoting on Giorgio’s insurance needs.

By pre-qualifying the risk herself, Angie determined which insurers to approach with this risk and has saved herself, and her client, time and effort.

4.2.2.2 Broker Contracts

As we learned in Chapter 2: Government Regulation and Insurance Contracts, an insurance brokerage is an independently owned business selling insurance policies on behalf of the insurance companies it represents. A registered insurance brokerage office in Ontario must have contracts to write business with at least two insurance companies.16 A brokerage is required to provide choice to consumers, but it does not automatically represent any and all insurance companies. The right to represent an insurer must be confirmed via a contractual agreement known as a broker contract.

A broker contract is a written agreement between an insurance company and a brokerage office. It states the scope of services to be provided by the brokerage and the compensation the brokerage will receive for providing such services.

There are two parties to a broker contract: the brokerage firm and the insurance company. Brokerage offices prefer to represent insurers who write a wide range of fairly priced general insurance products, and who are known for providing excellent claims service if a policyholder needs to submit a claim. For their part, insurance companies look for brokers who are well-known in their trading area, have a reputation for being honest and ethical, and consistently meet sales and profitability goals.

The contracts between brokers and insurance companies vary from one insurer to another. Let’s take a closer look at the major components of a typical agreement between an insurance company and a brokerage.

• Authority: Provides eligibility rules and guidelines related to the types of risks a brokerage is authorized to write and bind coverage for, and provides the coverage limits for each policy type.

• Ownership of policyholders’ business (expirations): States that the brokerage office retains ownership of clients’ files and controls the placement of insurance for those clients. Also makes clear which party owns the expirations and who will continue to provide coverage for these policyholders if the broker contract is terminated by either party.

• Commissions: Lists the commission schedule for each class of insurance sold and the terms of any contingent profit commission as a reward for any brokerage whose portfolio of business is profitable for the insurer.

• Billing procedures: States how premiums are to be remitted to the insurer, depending on the premium payment method offered.

• Claims handling: Indicates whether the brokerage is authorized to adjust and settle claims or not. If so, it states the types of claims and dollar amount the brokerage can handle.

• Termination: Spells out the terms for cancellation of the broker agreement by either party along with the length of the notice period (ranging from 90 to 180 days).

4.3 CLIENT COMMUNICATION

Though this course is not specific to office skills, you must understand the importance of documentation, active listening, and presentation. Proper note taking, active listening during your studies, and preparing to write the exam are skills that also translate into good office practices.

4.3.1 Documentation

As you learned in this chapter about minimizing E&O claims, the one fundamental thread to preventing this occurring is the accuracy of one’s documentation. Documentation means the written recording of actions, decisions, conversations, and written items between two or more parties. The premise of documentation is that it becomes an objective source material describing what took place between the involved parties if there is ever a question of what actually occurred. The subjective manner in which we all remember events cannot be trusted at the best of times, even less so when one person’s memory is compared to others. If the event in question is undergoing a claim process with an insurer or is in court being defended against, then only an objective written document is reliable.

As a broker, your documentation, or notes on a client profile, tells the story of what the client is doing, wants, decides, accepts, and rejects. This requires self-discipline to make these notes with every client interaction you have. These notes are often a key part of the claims process, as an insurer may request them as part of their internal processes. Or these notes can be used to defend you in an E&O claim against you and/or your brokerage.

Items to include in your notes are:

• Method of communication: call, email, text, letter, or fax.

• If communication was written, a copy of it needs to be attached to the note.

• The name of the individual communicated with.

• Policy change requests.

• Policy advice offered to client.

• Client response to advice (accept or reject).

• Disclosure of any coverages or coverage limits.

• Contact information of insurers if client needs to file a claim.

If there is one phrase to keep in mind about the importance of documentation in a broker’s role, it is; “If it isn’t written, it didn’t happen.”

4.3.2 Active Listening

There is a lot to be said about active listening, but for our purposes, it means to listen with exerted concentration to not only hear the words but also grasp the behavioural cues and attune oneself to the other person’s thoughts and feelings, and confirming that understanding with questions. Fundamentally, giving all your attention to what your client is doing and saying is the premise of active listening. It is not good enough to just be asking questions without equally being invested in capturing the replies.

As you learned earlier, a broker is a first-line underwriter, so gathering as complete a set of information and profile of the risk is one of the broker’s core duties. Mastering active listening is a vital skill of a successful broker as it can help reduce E&O claims and errors in application submissions, and help ensure there is consistency in your factual messaging to both clients and insurers.

4.3.3 Presentation

After all your application work is done, how you share the results of your application submissions can be an art form, depending on your client. Policy presentations are more common in the commercial client segment than in personal lines, but what your client would benefit from is always the best way to do it.

To be clear, the client makes the choice as to which policy is best for them. A broker’s job is to put on display all the vital items (coverages, endorsements, and limits) each policy contains for the client to comprehend and make an informed decision. The presentation should clearly display how detailed you incorporated your client’s true needs and how closely the policies you present have coverages and limits aligned to match those needs. Be prepared to answer questions in detail, but do so in plain language and include examples wherever the client is confused or needs help to fully understand. Some presentations can be done on paper, laid out for easy reading comparisons, which last a few minutes to an hour. Other presentations are delivered in person, have multiple stakeholders at the table, and can take several hours.

As you begin working in your brokerage, you will encounter many brokers going through the sales cycle with their clients, which will give you exposure to many best practices.

4.4 THE INSURANCE-BUYING PROCESS

While no two brokerages are identical, RIBO regulations obligate brokerages to create and implement office policies and procedures that will serve consumers in a fair, competent, and professional manner.

Remember: insurance is not a commodity. A commodity is a product or service that is indistinguishable from those sold or provided by competing companies. Grain, gasoline, or bandwidth are commodities, and they are sold primarily on the basis of price, rather than quality. While the cost of insurance is important to consumers, the insurance products and the services provided by an insurance broker are not commodities! Brokers can easily differentiate themselves from their competitors by consistently providing excellent client service and making valuable coverage recommendations. In many cases, insurance buyers will pay a little extra for an insurance policy if they perceive the value of the broker’s services.

When someone wishes to purchase an insurance policy from a brokerage firm, they will deal with an individual broker to complete the transaction. Selling insurance is much more than providing a quote, filling out an application, and taking payment. It’s about understanding the client’s requirements and recommending products or services appropriate for each individual client, as determined by a needs assessment. Performing a needs assessment is the mark of a professional insurance broker and distinguishes a broker’s services from the competition.

4.4.1 Needs Assessment

In its Code of Conduct Handbook, RIBO describes a needs assessment as “a process whereby the broker asks sufficient questions to understand the nature of the object of insurance or risk, and the client’s objectives in buying insurance.”17 The intricacy of the risk must also be taken into account.

Part of the needs assessment process is the ability to garner the information required to make both an accurate assessment and ultimately a proper quotation. The broker must obtain consent from the client before certain processes can take place. When the consent is asked for and given to the broker in writing, it is called “express consent”. A signature on the application is deemed express consent. This gives the broker the right to ask for and receive other parties’ relevant personal information which aids in the underwriting process.

When the “action” of asking for a quote is taken as tacit approval for the broker to ask for that information without a signature, that is called “implied consent”. Implied consent allows the broker to proceed with the quotation in a timely fashion, but it should be followed up with getting written consent.

In both cases, the information received is confidential and only to be used for the purposes of developing the quote.

For example, a client with a fleet of commercial-use vehicles (like an electrician company) would require an express consent to provide a more in-depth needs assessment. That client would sign an appropriate form, giving the broker the right to ask for underlying information. In contrast, a client with a single automobile used for commuting to and from work would typically start with implied consent, and if the quote is successful, the express consent would be completed by the signed application.

In many cases, the broker will physically visit a client’s property — especially when the risk is multi-faceted, such as a business, farm, or high-value home. Visiting the property allows the broker to pinpoint exposures to loss and the associated insurance requirements. For example, a client with a fleet of commercial-use vehicles (like an electrician company) would require a more in-depth needs assessment than a client with a single automobile used for commuting to and from work.

Next, the broker will calculate pricing, either using quotation software for straightforward risks or by contacting the underwriter for more complex commercial lines accounts. Because a broker represents several insurance companies, the client will expect the broker to provide more than one quote.

4.4.2 Present Coverage Options

Once the broker has analyzed the coverage options and price points of several insurers, they will present options to the client, along with recommendations for the best coverage matched to the client’s needs and budget. The broker must be prepared to explain the pros and cons of each to the prospective insurance buyer.

4.4.3 Complete Application Form

If the client wishes to proceed with an insurance purchase, they must complete and sign an application. Application forms include many questions. The broker must be able to give plain language explanations regarding the information requested on an application and to answer any questions the client might have along the way.

When completing the application form, remember to let the client provide the answers. The broker’s role is to assist the client when completing the application. However, the broker must not answer questions for the client. If the broker does this, and there is a future coverage dispute based on missing or incorrect information on the application, the insured can say it was the broker who gave the answers, not them. This could result in an E&O claim against the broker.

Also, never ask a client to sign a blank application form. Yes, completing an insurance application form is time consuming, and some clients may become impatient with the process. Since the broker has already gathered some information from the client when performing the needs assessment, it might seem harmless to have clients sign blank application forms so they can be on their way, and then fill in the client data afterwards. However, this is a dangerous and unethical practice that could result in disputes over misrepresentation of information, rejection of future claims, or even accusations of insurance fraud by the broker.

One best practice is to explain to the client the time commitment involved. Let them know how taking the time now will ensure accuracy and avoid problems down the road, if the client needs to make an insurance claim one day.

4.4.4 Apply for Insurance

Is the following statement true or false? When an applicant knowingly makes a false statement on an insurance application to get a lower premium, no one gets hurt.

False! This is insurance fraud and it hurts honest policyholders by driving up the cost of insurance for everyone.

When applying for insurance, the applicant has a legal duty to be forthright and honest. In fact, intentionally providing false information on an insurance application is a form of insurance fraud and can lead to denial of a claim, large fines, or even a criminal record.

4.4.4.1 Preventing Fraud on Applications

Brokers can, and should, play an important role in preventing insurance fraud. Most standard insurance application forms include a statement warning an applicant against knowingly making false or misleading statements on the application form and the consequences of doing so. We recommend that brokers read this statement aloud to each applicant before asking them to sign the application form.

We’ll look at the application process in greater detail in Chapter 7: Property Insurance and Chapter 9: Automobile Insurance.

4.4.5 Binding Coverage

In Chapter 3: Government Regulation and Insurance Contracts, we learned that a binder is a written or oral agreement giving temporary insurance protection to the insured. Binding coverage means the brokerage, as an authorized representative of the insurance company, has the authority to confirm insurance coverage is in force as soon as the client has agreed to purchase a policy. The client receives proof of insurance immediately, without waiting for the insurer to issue and mail policy documents.

For the average home or automobile policy, the brokerage can usually bind coverage right on the spot. Binding coverage for clients is a common task performed by individual brokers employed by a brokerage office.

4.4.5.1 Broker Binding Authority

As mentioned earlier in this chapter, a brokerage office receives its authority to bind coverage on behalf of an insurance company via contractual agreement with each insurer the brokerage represents. Binding authority lays out the types of risks a brokerage is authorized to bind, the eligibility rules, and coverage limits for each risk category.

Note: Each individual broker employed by a brokerage is bound by the terms of the binding authority agreement for each insurance company their brokerage represents.

Binding authority is subject to change based on market conditions or significant loss events. To allow for changes to broker binding authority without the need to renegotiate broker agreements, the authority clause in a broker contract generally refers to the binding authority as found in the broker underwriting rules manuals.

The following is an example of a broker binding authority:

No binder may be issued for a period greater than 30 days
Residential dwelling and contents: $1,000,000 	Image 	Limits will vary based on the local municipal fire protection.
Commercial building and contents: $2,000,000

Note: Binding authority varies by insurer. It is the broker’s responsibility to know the binding authority for each insurer represented.

4.4.5.1.1 Do Not Exceed Binding Authority

If a broker exceeds the limits of an insurer’s binding authority and a loss occurs, the broker and the brokerage may find themselves facing an E&O claim. It will be the brokerage, not the insurer, who will be responsible to the client for the amount of the loss!

If a broker is in doubt regarding their binding authority with an insurer, check the insurer’s underwriting manual. If binding authority is still unclear, the broker should speak with their manager or contact the company’s underwriter.

4.4.6 Premium Payment

There are several ways a client can pay the policy premium when purchasing a new policy from a brokerage, including:

• In full or through premium financing plans offered by an insurer (a down payment may be required).

• By cash, cheque, or, if offered by the brokerage firm, debit or credit card.

• Third-party premium financing plans.

4.4.7 Broker as Fiduciary

In Chapter 3: Government Regulation and Insurance Contracts, we learned a fiduciary is someone who takes care of someone else’s money or other assets. When a broker receives insurance premiums from policyholders, they are acting as a fiduciary for the client. In turn, premiums collected from clients, net of brokerage commission, must be paid to the insurance company. This means the broker is acting as a fiduciary for the insurance company.

All brokerage offices and individual brokers have a legal and ethical responsibility to look after the funds in a prescribed manner. Additional information regarding the holding of policyholder’s premiums in trust will be provided in Chapter 6: The Regulation of Insurance Brokers.

4.5 RENEWING POLICIES

Insurance policies are typically in force for a 12-month term. At expiration of the term, the policy can either be renewed for another 12 months or allowed to expire or lapse. A lapsed policy is one that has reached its expiry date and has not been renewed or extended.

The Insurance Bureau of Canada defines a renewal as a certificate that attests to the fact that an insurance policy, or contract of insurance, has been extended for another term. The full policy wording (the fine print) is not attached to the renewal certificate unless the insurance company has modified the policy coverage since the policy was first written.

Most policies for personal use automobiles, residential property, and small business packages are automatically renewed by the insurance company. Before renewing more complex commercial and farm policies, the insurance company underwriter will require updated information about the risk to ensure it still meets the insurer’s eligibility criteria.

Although many policies are automatically renewed, renewal time is an opportunity for the broker to re-assess the client’s risk. Are there any new drivers on the automobile policy? Has the insured acquired any high-value jewellery in the past year, or started a home-based business? Is there a change to a farm or business operation increasing the exposure to loss?

4.5.1 Profitability of Renewal Business

Writing new business is absolutely critical. However, policy renewals are the major source of income for brokerages. The upfront costs of acquiring new clients are high. These costs can only be recaptured when clients remain with the brokerage. The longer clients are retained by the brokerage, the more profitable the brokerage will be.

The Pareto Principle, also known as the 80/20 Rule, states that for many events, roughly 80% of effects come from 20% of causes. Applied to client retention, this means 80% of future income will come from 20% of current clients. Why? Because satisfied long-term clients will:

• Refer others to the business.

• Respond to marketing efforts.

• Buy more products and services over time.

80% of your future business comes from 20% of your existing customers!

The most successful brokers are those with a high rate of client retention and who view renewal time as an opportunity to reconnect with clients and to maintain the relationship.

This brings us to the end of the insurance purchasing process. Now, what does a broker do when a claim comes in?

4.6 HANDLING CLAIMS

When a loss occurs, policyholders expect prompt, efficient handling of their claim. Insurance companies prefer claims be reported in one of two ways:

1) Policyholders report a claim directly to the insurance company via a toll-free phone number or an online reporting site.

2) Policyholders report a claim to the broker office, who in turn reports the claim to the insurance company.

Some insurance companies employ both methods: a toll-free reporting centre for small, straightforward claims (such as a cracked car windshield) and broker reporting for medium to large claims.

Before we review the procedures for taking claims at a broker office, the following terms must be understood.

• Claimant: An insured person who makes an insurance claim. The claimant is usually the policyholder, but it can be anyone who is defined as an insured person under the policy.

• Adjuster: The person who reviews and settles claims on behalf of the insurance company. The adjuster could be an employee of the insurance company or an independent contractor hired by the company.

• Adjusting: The process of reviewing and settling losses under an insurance policy.

4.6.1 Taking Claims at the Brokerage Office

When a policyholder experiences a loss they believe is covered by their insurance policy, the claim needs to be reported and adjusted. Unless specifically authorized to do so, the brokerage does not settle claims with claimants. This is the role of the insurance adjuster.

Larger brokerage offices may have one or more brokers dedicated to taking claim reports from clients and submitting these reports to the appropriate insurance company. In other offices, claims reports are taken by the first available broker or the broker who normally handles the client’s account.

When someone calls the brokerage to report a claim, it is important to remember that the people involved — the clients — will be understandably upset. No matter how insignificant the claim may seem to brokerage personnel, the occurrence is very important to the client. Good claims service begins by treating each claimant with sympathy, empathy, and concern.

GLOSSARY

• Active listening: A method of listening to another person with exerted concentration to not only hear the words but also grasp the behavioural cues and attune oneself to the other person’s thoughts and feelings and confirming this understanding with questions.

• Adjuster: The person who reviews and settles claims on behalf of the insurance company. The adjuster could be an employee of the insurance company or an independent contractor hired by the company.

• Adjusting: The process of reviewing and settling losses under an insurance policy.

• Binding coverage: A process where the brokerage, as an authorized representative of the insurance company, has the authority to confirm immediately that coverage is in force.

• Broker binding authority: The power given to a brokerage under a contractual agreement allowing the brokerage to immediately provide insurance coverage to an applicant up to a pre-agreed limit without first receiving approval from an underwriter.

• Broker contract: A written agreement between an insurance company and a brokerage office. It states the scope of services to be provided by the brokerage and the compensation the brokerage will receive for providing such services.

• Claimant: An insured person who makes a claim.

• Declaration: The portion of the insurance policy containing a summary of mandatory information, such as the name and address of the insured, the policy period, a description and location of the property insured, the amount of insurance coverage, the insurable interest of others (if any), and the policy premium.

• Errors and Omissions (E&O): A type of professional liability insurance that protects companies, their workers, and other professionals against claims of inadequate work or negligent actions.

• Express consent: The act where a person gives declarative permission for a specific action to be taken by the other party to which they both agreed. Also known as informed consent.

• Implied consent: The assumption that a person has given permission for an action, which is inferred from their actions, rather than expressly or explicitly provided.

• Lapsed policy: A policy that has reached its expiry date and has not been renewed or extended.

• Needs assessment: A process whereby the broker asks sufficient questions to understand the nature of the object of insurance or risk and the client’s objectives in buying insurance.

• Renewal: A certificate that attests to the fact that an insurance policy, or contract of insurance, has been extended for another term.
