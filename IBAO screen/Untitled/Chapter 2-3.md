2.1 INTRODUCTION

In Chapter 1: Introduction to General Insurance, we looked at the basic concepts of insurance: the principle of indemnity, risks, and hazards. In this chapter, we’ll look at how insurance is organized and sold. We’ll also compare the roles of broker and agent. Finally, we’ll examine how the private and public entities that create the insurance policies (or products) function.

2.2 WHO WRITES INSURANCE?

The act of selling or issuing an insurance policy is known as writing insurance. Canadians can purchase insurance either directly from an insurance company (direct) or through an intermediary (agents or brokers). Let’s look at each of these in detail.

2.2.1 Direct Writers

As the name suggests, a direct writer sells its insurance products directly to customers, typically through centralized call centres and online. The sales agents are employees of the insurance company and are limited to selling only those insurance products offered by the insurer. The insurance company owns all of the business written and performs all administration functions. TD Insurance and Belair Direct are examples of direct writers.

2.2.2 Intermediaries

An intermediary is a person or organization who acts as a mediator or link between two parties. Travel agents, investment brokers, and real estate brokers are common examples of intermediaries in the business world. In the insurance business, there are two types of intermediaries: agents and brokers. These two terms are often used interchangeably within the classification of insurance intermediaries, but there are key differences between them:

• Insurance agents: Agents are authorized to sell the products of one insurance company only and, in doing so, they represent the interests of the insurance company. In Ontario, agents are regulated and licensed by a different regime than insurance brokers. Some agents are employed by direct writers, while others work for independent agencies.

• Insurance brokers: Brokers primarily represent the interest of the policyholder in an insurance transaction, and they can sell the insurance products of more than one insurance company. Brokers perform their work at brokerage firms.

2.2.2.1 Independent Agencies

Independent insurance agencies are independent businesses, typically owned by one or more individual agents under an exclusive arrangement with a sponsoring insurance company. This means they sell insurance for one insurer only. An independent agency owns the portfolio of business it generates. The commissions the agency earns from the insurer are used to pay office expenses.

The Co-operators Group is an example of an insurance company that offers its products to consumers through a national network of independent agencies.

2.2.2.2 Independent Brokerages

There are several thousand independent insurance brokerage offices in Canada, employing more than 35,000 individual insurance brokers.3

Brokerages are independently owned businesses. In most cases, insurance brokerages sell insurance policies from two or more insurance companies. The brokerage owns the business it produces and is responsible for providing its clients with a number of services. In return, the brokerage is paid commissions by the insurers whose policies it sells. These commissions are used to pay operating expenses, including salaries and/or commission to its employees.

With few exceptions, consumer protection regulations set out in the Registered Insurance Brokers Act require all persons working in a brokerage to hold a broker license. We’ll learn more about brokerage operations, and the role of the individual brokers who work in them, in Chapter 4: The Insurance-Buying Process.

2.3 Types of Insurance Companies

In Canada, there are two types of insurance companies: government insurers and private insurers. We’ll learn about each of these in the sections below.

2.3.1 Government Insurers

Federal and provincial governments are involved in providing various insurance plans, including medical insurance, employment insurance, and workers’ compensation.

The governments of British Columbia, Saskatchewan, and Manitoba operate compulsory automobile insurance systems in their respective provinces. All vehicle owners in these provinces are required to purchase mandatory third-party liability insurance coverage from the government. In most cases, additional insurance — such as collision coverage and excess liability limits — may be purchased from private insurers or from the government.

In the province of Quebec, the government manages the automobile insurance regime for bodily injury insurance only.

The Saskatchewan government also operates an insurance division, offering all forms of property and casualty (P&C) insurance through SGI Canada. SGI’s products are sold through a network of independent insurance brokers in several provinces, including Ontario.4

2.3.2 Private Insurers

Most insurance policies in Canada are written by private insurers, the most common of which are stock insurance companies and mutual insurance companies. Both private and mutual insurers have the same basic function: to write or sell insurance policies to those who require insurance. The main difference between the two is ownership. 2.3.2.1 Stock Insurance Companies

A stock insurance company is a business entity that is owned by its shareholders. The money to operate a stock insurer may come from private funds or through publicly traded stock. The main goal of a stock company is to maximize shareholder value. Any profits earned by a stock insurer are reinvested in the company or paid to shareholders in the form of dividends.

Stock insurers may be wholly Canadian owned or may be a Canadian subsidiary of a company whose home base is in another country. Either way, most stock companies are licensed to transact business in all provinces, although some prefer to maintain a provincial or regional presence only.

Most insurance policies written in Canada today are provided by stock companies. Most brokerage offices represent several stock insurers. Intact and AVIVA are two examples of stock insurance companies.

2.3.2.2 Mutual Insurance Companies

A mutual insurance company is a corporation owned by its policyholders. The main goal of a mutual insurer is to collect sufficient premiums from its policyholders to cover the cost of paying claims and operating the company. There are no shareholders, so any profits generated by a mutual company are either kept in reserve, re-invested in the company, or distributed back to policyholders in the form of lower premiums (or sometimes in a refund of premiums).

According to the Canadian Association of Mutual Insurance Companies (CAMIC), some of Canada’s very first insurance companies were mutual companies set up in the early 1800s to insure farms.5 Today, some mutual insurers operate on a national basis, while others do business within one province only, or within a specific geographic region of a province.

Wawanesa Mutual is an example of a mutual insurer operating nationally, while Trillium Mutual is an example of a mutual insurer operating in the province of Ontario only.

2.3.3 Jurisdiction

There is a distinction between the federal or provincial regulations that define licensing to be along provincial boundaries. These boundaries, or jurisdictions, are what brokers must align with. Every insurance entity operating in Canada, whether company or brokerage, is bound by the rules of the jurisdiction where the business resides or does business in. For example, if a brokerage is located in BC, then it must adhere to BC regulations, and by extension, it can only sell products to other BC residents (personal and commercial). The brokerage must also maintain licensing and file financial reports to its regulator.

In Ontario, FSRA regulates the companies and agents, and RIBO regulates the brokers. Many Ontario brokerages operate either in their local trading area or within the province. As a result, they only need to make the bi-annual filing with RIBO. However, for brokerages who operate in multiple jurisdictions, they must also comply with the local regulations and filing rules.

Similarly, a larger brokerages that operates in several provinces would need an in-house broker to maintain a licence in the jurisdiction where it needs to do business. The reason is directly related to its client/customer base; if its clients and customers reside, do business or have property in another province, then the brokerage will need jurisdictionally licensed brokers to maintain full service to those clients.

For example, if a client has a house in Ottawa and a chalet in the Laurentians, this would require both an Ontario-licensed broker for the house and a Quebec-licensed broker for the chalet in that same brokerage. If coverage is placed with one insurer for both locations, the insurer too must also be licensed to do business in both jurisdictions or be federally licensed. Unfortunately, at this time, there is not one national or inter-jurisdictional licence for brokers.

2.4 MARKET CYCLES

Insurance is a part of the financial industry, and money (or capital) flows for insurance in just as many ways as it does for banks and stock markets.. There are waves of easy and cheap access to capital, and waves when capital becomes expensive and restricted. In insurance, these changes in access to capital directly affects the premium costs (see Section 2.5 Rake Making) because the ability of insurers to pay for future losses is contingent upon their ability to have enough capital to pay claims from all their current policies. At the end of the day, insurance is a business, and profits have a direct impact on an insurer’s ability to continue. These market cycles in insurance are commonly known hard markets and soft markets. Let’s look at each of them.

2.4.1 Hard Market

Let’s start with the hard market. A hard market comes when there is restriction on the amount of capital available to insurers to buy so they can take on more risk at the same or similar rates. Insurers face difficulty in taking on new policyholders as well as maintaining the same premium customers have to pay for their policies. This tightening creates more aggressive competition amongst customers and policyholders to find the best rates for their insurance. For brokers, this means there are often more restrictions on what types of risks their insurers are willing to accept, and a reduction in the number of insurers available for certain insurance types. In a hard market, brokers typically have to shop a customer to more insurers than usual to find rates, coverages, and limits that their customer needs and can afford.

2.4.2 Soft Market

Conversely, a soft market is when there is a surplus of capital, giving insurers easier access to increase their capacity and take on more policyholders. In this cycle, the competition is between insurers to either reduce premiums, offer more coverages or higher limits, and to also widen the parameters of who would be an acceptable risk. This is a period where customers have greater choice. In a soft market, brokers often find it much easier to get policies for customers. They also often see a loosening of restrictions on applications being submitted and better discounts or rates on the premiums.

2.5 RATE MAKING

To start this brief section, you need to be aware that rate making has many more complexities than what is described here. We are simplifying this explanation because your Level 1 exam does not assess you on the specifics of rate making..

Insurance companies determine how much they will charge for a particular risk based on an equation made up of the “rate” per dollar value of the item being insured multiplied by the “value” of that item. Rate making is the process of analyzing what rates, or premiums, should be charged for insurance.

When determining the value of a house, the industry uses a software tool called the Home Evaluation Calculator, an independent source to value homes. To evaluate an item such as jewelry, a proper, qualified independent jewelry appraisal is required. All the numbers used to generate these values must be verifiable, to avoid any guesswork.

The insurer’s actuaries use mathematical calculations and projections to determine the rate. These calculations are based on industry and company claim results and the industry outlook for future losses. There are many components in the calculation. The rates that companies use can vary based on construction, fire protection, urban density, materials, geography, changing weather patterns, and their impact on claims. For example, an increase in fire-resistive buildings and improved flood mitigation in a location allows for the calculation of lower rates, whereas an increase in expected wildfire losses from drought pushes rates higher. Brokers in different parts of the country may have different rates for the same type of building. The higher the risk the higher the rate; the lower the risk, the lower the rate.

The actuaries also calculate the amount of premium that an insurance company needs to generate to cover expected losses. This is called pure premium, the base amount of the premium calculated to create the pool of money to cover expected losses. It does not consider profit margins and other expenses. Pure premium is only calculated by actuaries within the insurance company. There are many other expenses in the operation of an insurance company that must be considered, such as company operation costs and commissions. These are added to the pure premium.

If the final rate figure used to calculate premium is too low, the company may write a lot of business, but a year of unusual claims could put the company into a negative balance (see Section 2.6 Loss Ratio below). Conversely, if the rate is too high, making policy premiums non-competitive with the marketplace, the insurer would not achieve its sales goal and would then potentially not be profitable. Rates are not fixed and can be adjusted and amended within reason as required by the marketplace.

2.6 LOSS RATIO

Before we move on to the roles and departments within an insurance company, it’s important to pause here to discuss how insurances companies earn a living. You have learned how and why money is available to insurance companies to pay for claims by the insureds, from premiums to reinsurance. However, insurance companies must carefully manage their expenses so a potentially bad run of multiple high claims does not overrun their capacity. Insurance companies are not charities; they need to make money and earn profits to succeed in their business.

Let’s look at two key ways that insurance companies manage their finances. The amount of money an insurer has available is proportionally measured against how much money it spends on losses. This calculation, called loss ratio, is a calculation derived by dividing the amount of incurred losses by the amount of premium earned over a specified period expressed as a percentage of earned premiums. A loss ratio does not include any expenses incurred by the insurer. This ratio is calculated by dividing the total amount of claims by the earned premiums, then multiplying that number by 100. Loss ratios for brokers are usually monitored by their insurers based on their portfolios with that insurer. Some broker agreements offer brokers an incentive for maintaining a profitable book of business.

2.8 OTHER INSURANCE MARKETS

Conventional P&C insurers write insurance for a wide variety of exposures to loss. However, there are certain higher-risk operations that the average private or mutual insurer is not prepared to underwrite. Instead, a broker may need to seek coverage from a specialty market, such as Lloyd’s of London or a managing general agent.

2.8.1 Lloyd’s of London

Lloyd’s of London is not an insurance company and it does not transact any insurance business itself. Lloyd’s can be best described as a marketplace for insurance buyers and sellers. It can be a valuable option for brokers looking for a market to write large, high-risk operations, such as commercial aircraft, satellite risks, oil rigs, and nuclear power plant facilities. All of these items are written by Lloyd’s underwriters, along with other unusual risks, like the voices of opera singers, the legs of movie stars, and the chance an outdoor entertainment event may be cancelled due to bad weather.

Lloyd’s of London was established in the late 17th century, when London’s importance as a trade centre led to an increasing demand for ship and cargo insurance. Although he was never an insurer himself, Edward Lloyd’s coffee shop was a popular gathering place for ship owners and captains returning from overseas voyages. Seeing opportunity, Lloyd began renting out tables (known as “boxes”) to entrepreneurial businessmen to sell marine insurance to ship owners. Decades later, the coffee shop had become recognized as the place to obtain marine insurance. The modern Lloyd’s was born in 1774, when it moved to the Royal Exchange building in London’s financial district. Despite the move, Lloyd’s purpose remains the same: to provide a space where insurance buyers and sellers transact business.

Today, Lloyd’s can be viewed as a mall where brokers can shop at the desks (boxes) of various syndicates. These syndicates consist of companies or individuals who basically function as insurance companies that offer a specific type of insurance6. Syndicates can come together for a short or long term as a group of individuals, or for a long term as a corporate structure. These syndicates are often the primary innovators of new coverages and are often the first to respond to how to deal with crisis in the world. A managing agent manages the day-to-day activities of the desk on behalf of the syndicate. Each syndicate has its own expert underwriters for each class of business it chooses to write.

The insurance buyer goes to a broker who is accredited by Lloyd’s to operate in the building. The broker contacts a managing agent/underwriter to arrange for cover on the specific risk. Once coverage is arranged, the underwriter signs the slip on behalf of the syndicate confirming coverage. More than one syndicate may have to participate in an insurance contract (a subscription policy, see Note.) thereby spreading the risk among multiple syndicates. If multiple syndicates are required, each signs the slip under the original, signer creating a policy that is “underwritten” by all the participants.

The policy to be issued can be either a “stock” or “off the shelf” policy with adjustments made by standard industry endorsements or a tailored manuscript policy. The policy form is dependent upon the intricacies of the elements of the risk. If the risk is very complex or unique and does not fit the norm, a manuscript policy is crafted specific for that individual risk. The elements of this type of policy must be agreed to by all those subscribing to the policy and the policyholder.

Lloyd’s uses coverholders to give managing agents a broader scope, increase efficiency and expand its market. Coverholders are independent companies throughout the world that managing agents authorize to enter insurance contracts to be underwritten by syndicates. Working on behalf of syndicates, coverholders allow Lloyd’s syndicates to operate globally in a cost-effective way without having to set up their own shops at many locations. Coverholders have the authority to bind coverage, issue documents, collect premiums, settle claims and generally write business on a discretionary basis within very tight limits. They may, for example, be specialists in marine, directors & officers or cyber insurance.

Collectively, Lloyd’s syndicates insure risks totalling more than £46.7 billion ($80.5 billion) in insurance premiums each year7. In 2024, there were 77 syndicates, 380 brokers and 3,434 coverholders operating under the Lloyd’s banner8. The Lloyd’s corporation has had a Canadian presence for nearly 200 years9. Canadian brokers and agents contact coverholders domiciled in Canada to arrange the placement of insurance for their clients. Brokers with very large risks often personally travel to London to meet with Lloyd’s brokers to arrange coverage. Lloyd’s truly is a global market.

Note: A subscription policy is one that a group of insurers have agreed to participate in with one company taking the lead.

For example, a commercial client has a risk valued at $2M. The broker approaches an insurer, Intact, who cannot accept the full capacity and offers to take 50% of it. Intact, for example, might not be able to accept the full $2M capacity, so instead offers to take 50% of it. The broker then approaches another insurer, such as Wawanesa, who accepts 25% of the remaining capacity. With only 75% of the total capacity covered, the broker approaches a third insurer, Definity. They agree to accept the remaining 25% of capacity. In this example, Intact would be the lead company and the policy would be issued on their wording to the client; the two additional insurers are subscribing to that policy.

2.8.2 Managing General Agents

A managing general agent (MGA) is a type of insurance brokerage that has been granted authority by one or more insurance companies to transact business on their behalf.

Why would an insurance company choose to use an MGA? One reason is to allow the insurance company to participate in writing higher-risk operations, without needing to have in-house underwriting expertise to adequately assess such operations. Consequently, MGAs tend to be specialists in specific classes of business, such as sports liability, professional liability, contractors involved in dangerous work like demolition or blasting, and more.

An MGA can be described as “a broker for a broker,” because a client will approach a broker and the broker will arrange coverage with the MGA. However, an MGA may also deal directly with a client, as long as the MGA and its staff have the correct licenses.

2.8.3 Insurance Bureau of Canada

Property and casualty insurance companies, including reinsurance companies, are members of an industry association called the Insurance Bureau of Canada (IBC). The IBC is composed of large and small members, both stock and mutual companies. Its purpose is to deal with insurance matters, issues, and advocacy for the industry with the government and other stakeholders. It creates insurance forms, collects valuable data from many sources, and collates that data to provide valuable information for its members and the public. For its members, it provides insurance and VIN validation. For the public, it provides safety tips and information about flood, fire, hail, and earthquake10.

2.9 CONCLUSION

This brings us to the end of our chapter on how insurance is organized in Canada. Insurance is a global industry that is organized locally. As we’ve learned, Canadians have many choices when it comes to purchasing their insurance: directly or through an intermediary; through agencies or brokerages; through stock insurance companies, mutual insurance companies, or the government; and even through a specialty insurer such as Lloyd’s of London or a managing general agent. The more you learn about these different options, the better you’ll be able to help your clients make the choice that’s right for them.

GLOSSARY

• Actuary: A professional with advanced mathematical skills who deals with the measurement and management of risk and uncertainty.

• Broker portal: A software program downloaded to the broker policy management system that allows brokers to upload and download policy information data to a specific insurance company’s portal.

• Combined ratio: The total of the expenses ratio and loss ratio of an insurer over a specified period of time.

• Direct writer: An insurance company that sells policies directly to customers, typically through call centres and online.

• Earned premium: The difference between premium paid by the insured and the portion the insurance company must return to them if the policy is cancelled during its term. The earned premium is the amount that pays for the coverage furnished by the insurance company during the expired portion of the policy term.

• Hard market: A hard market occurs when low profit margins force out some insurers of the competition. The remaining insurers have enough business to enable them to be selective about the kinds of property they insure. Insurers generally return to a more disciplined underwriting approach in a hard market, and the insured normally receives little or no rate discounts.

• Insurance agency: An independent business selling insurance policies through an exclusive agreement with one insurance company.

• Insurance agent: An individual authorized to sell products for one insurance company only and, in doing so, primarily represents the interests of the company.

• Insurance broker: A person who primarily represents the interest of the policyholder in an insurance transaction and who can sell their clients the policies of more than one insurance company.

• Insurance brokerage: An independent business selling insurance policies through an agreement with two or more insurance companies.

• Insurance Bureau of Canada (IBC): An industry association composed of large and small members, both stock and mutual companies, mandated to deal with insurance matters, issues, and advocacy for the industry with the government and other stakeholders.

• Intermediary: A person or organization who acts as a mediator or link between two parties. An insurance broker is an example of an intermediary between an insurance company and an applicant for insurance.

• Lloyd’s of London: A marketplace where insurance buyers and sellers conduct business. Lloyd’s is not an insurance company.

• Loss ratio: Proportion between earned premiums and loss payments, including adjustment expense, made over a specified period.

• Managing general agent: A type of brokerage that has been granted authority by one or more insurance companies to transact business on their behalf.

• Manuscript policy: A policy form or wording which does not conform to standard wordings in general use and which is often unique to the risk being undertaken. Usually developed by the insurance company.

• Mutual insurance company: An insurance company owned by its policyholders with a goal of covering costs. Any profit is re-invested in the company or distributed back to policyholders.

• Pure premium: That part of the premium needed to pay losses, excluding the portion required by the company for its expenses.

• Rate making: The actuaries’ compiling and analyzing of data to establish rates that accurately reflect the level of risk.

• Soft market: This is characterized by intense competition between insurers; as a result, rates tend to be low.

• Stock insurance company: A business entity owned by its shareholders with a goal of making a profit for them.

• Subscription policy: A policy in which a group of insurers have agreed to participate, with one company taking the lead.

• Underwriter: The person at the insurance company who analyzes application forms submitted by brokers to ensure applicants’ risks meet the insurance company’s established eligibility criteria. This also applies to Travel Health Insurance.

• Unearned premium: The portion of the premium that can be returned to the insured upon policy cancellation.

• Writing insurance: The act of selling or issuing an insurance policy.

3.1 INTRODUCTION

Insurance protects financial assets; therefore, it is fiduciary in nature. In simple terms, a fiduciary is someone who handles other peoples’ money.

Insurers and brokers are both fiduciaries and must ensure that the trust placed in them is not abused. Consumers expect the premiums they pay will be forwarded by the broker to the insurance company. In addition, consumers expect that insurers will retain a portion of all premiums collected to pay claims, or that they will provide refunds if a policy is cancelled mid-term.

In this chapter, we’ll review some of the legal and formal processes in place to administer the insurance industry, and to regulate and construct insurance contracts, in the Canadian context.

3.2 GOVERNMENT REGULATION

To protect Canadian insurance consumers, the government must regulate insurers. The conduct of the general insurance industry in Canada is closely supervised and regulated by federal and provincial statutes. Below are the four levels of insurance regulation in Canada.

Image

3.2.1 Federal Supervision

The Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) supervises Canadian insurance companies incorporated under the Insurance Companies Act as well as foreign insurers who are licensed to sell insurance products in Canada. OSFI’s main role is to review and monitor all federally licensed insurers to determine their financial soundness.

3.2.1.1 The Property and Casualty Insurance Compensation Corporation

Despite a strong monitoring process, insurance company failures can occur. In order to protect policyholders and claimants from such an event, federal government regulators approved the formation of the Property and Casualty Insurance Compensation Corporation (PACICC) in 1992.

Membership in PACICC is mandated by legislation and regulation for property and casualty insurers who write automobile, property, liability, and most other property and casualty (P&C) insurance policies. In the unlikely event of the collapse of a P&C insurer in Canada, the industry-funded PACICC will respond to policyholder claims.11

3.2.1.2 Canadian Council of Insurance Regulators

The Canadian Council of Insurance Regulators (CCIR) is an interjurisdictional association of insurance regulators whose mandate is to facilitate and promote an efficient and effective regulatory system in Canada to serve the public interest. It is working to harmonize regulations across Canada and to find solutions to common regulatory problems and issues. Its membership is made up of Superintendents of Insurance from each province and the federal government.12

3.2.2 Provincial Supervision

For consumer protection, it is critical that insurance companies remain solvent, or financially sound. An insolvent insurance company is unable to meet its financial obligations, including paying expenses, salaries, commissions, or claims made by policyholders.

The Financial Services Regulatory Authority of Ontario (FSRA) regulates insurers operating in Ontario and enforces the provincial Insurance Act and the Compulsory Automobile Insurance Act. FSRA is responsible for:

• Licensing of both life and general insurers and agents.

• Monitoring insurer solvency.

• Ensuring fair marketing practices.

• Approving automobile insurance coverage and rates.

3.2.2.1 Fair Treatment of Customers

Adopted by RIBO and FSRA (at the provincial level) and Canadian Insurance Services Regulatory Organizations (CISRO) (at the national level), Fair Treatment of Customers is a set of guidelines for insurance companies and insurance intermediaries (that is, brokers). It comprises a common set of business conduct expectations to ensure every customer is treated fairly throughout the time a customer is on a policy with them. Since customers may not be knowledgeable about the insurance product they have and/or how their insurance will protect them, these guidelines set out how all parties to the policy should act to protect the customer from unfairness, incorrect products, and discriminatory actions or procedures.

Some of the guidelines include:

• Know your client;

• Educating your client;

• Conflict and commission disclosure; and

• Protection of personal information.

3.2.2.1.1 Fact Sheet About Your Broker

RIBO went a little further with the Fair Treatment of Customers guidelines when they created standards for a document called Fact Sheet about Your Broker, which all RIBO brokerages must provide to every customer. This document explains what insurance is and how it works, including the insurance process, responsibilities of the broker, and responsibilities of the customer.

3.2.2.1.2 Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act

Canada’s Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act (PIPEDA) provides protection for the personal data of all Canadians. It does not apply in BC, Alberta, or Quebec, which have their own provincial regulations. It applies to all commercial for-profit businesses. The purpose of the law is to regulate the collection, storage, and use of personal data in the course of business activities. The Privacy Commissioner oversees the operations of the Act and is responsible for protecting and promoting privacy rights, and businesses are responsible to the Commissioner for contraventions of the Act. The penalties for contravention are fines up to $100,000. All breaches of the Act must be reported to the Commissioner.

Some of the principles for collecting, protecting, and disclosing information are:

• Accountability: someone in each office is responsible for implementing the provisions of the Act and ensuring the office is compliant with the Act.

• Customer consent must be obtained for each transaction.

• Only collect data that is necessary and keep it only so long as is necessary. It must be properly disposed of when no longer required.

• All information must be protected physically or electronically.

• All employees must be aware of office practices regarding the Act.

• The client must be advised why the information is being collected.

Clients have a right to see the information that has been collected. That information must be readily available to the client in a usable manner upon request. The Act gives rights to individuals to control their personal information, and they have the right to withdraw that information13.

Note: PIPEDA is anticipated to be replaced by a newer privacy act, the Consumer Privacy Protection Act (CPPA). CPPA has passed second reading (12/2023) and, as of writing this textbook, is now before a committee for review. It is expected to be approved in the near future.

3.3 INSURANCE CONTRACTS

Contracts are part of everyday life — for example, you’ve likely encountered lease agreements or contracts for the purchase of real estate. Those are classic examples of written contracts, known as express contracts.

Many jurisdictions require a written contract for some agreements such as land sales or contracts of high monetary value. These contracts form a written, legally binding agreement between two parties, where all the details are spelled out. Both parties intentionally enter into the contract and clearly understand that the contract requires them to perform certain obligations. In other words, they have a mutual intent to be bound by the agreement. The contract also states the effective date when the agreement will begin for both, or either, parties.

There are also oral and non-written contracts which are called implied contracts. There does not need to be an exchange of words that specify the agreement, but it can be reasonably inferred from the party’s behaviour or the surrounding circumstances that the parties have a tacit understanding of having formed an agreement. The details of the implied contract are more ambiguous and may only have generalities in its details.

An action such as ordering a meal at a drive thru window or booking a taxi or ride-sharing service are examples of an implied contracts. They have the same legal implications as a written or express contract and are just as legally binding and enforceable as the express contract.

Insurance policies are essentially formal (express) written contracts where insurance companies agree to assume the risk of others. In this chapter, the terms ‘contract” and “policy” are used interchangeably.

Note: Brokers are the intermediaries between the insurance company and the party wishing to purchase insurance. This means they are responsible for creating a legally enforceable contract. As a broker, it is critical that you fully understand all contract elements and are able to apply contract theory to the everyday work of arranging insurance contracts for your clients.

3.3.1 Eight Elements Required for Any Insurance Contract

3.3.1.1 Five Elements of Contracts in General

If there is a dispute regarding the terms of a contract after the fact, a court of law may review the contract to determine if it was valid in the first place. To be enforceable by law, all contracts require that these five elements must be present:

1) Agreement.

2) Consideration.

3) Legality of object.

4) Legal capacity of the parties to the contract.

5) Genuine intention.

Let’s take a closer look at each of these elements.

3.3.1.1.1 Agreement

The first test of contract enforceability is proving an agreement has been reached — that there was a meeting of the minds regarding the terms of the contract. There must be an offer made, and an unconditional acceptance of the terms of that offer. For example, a completed application for insurance constitutes an offer to the insurer. The offer is accepted when the insurer issues a policy that matches the offer.

However, there is no assumed agreement while the parties are still negotiating contract terms. For example, if the insurer wants to change the terms of the offer by issuing a policy with different coverage limits than requested on the application form, there is no agreement until, and unless, the applicant agrees to the new terms.

It’s common practice for contracts to be written to avoid misunderstandings; however, an oral agreement is just as legally binding on the parties to the contract as a written agreement is.

Image

3.3.1.1.2 Consideration

A legally enforceable contract requires an exchange of something of value. This is known as “consideration.” In other words, consideration is evidence the two parties intend to be bound by the contract.

In an insurance contract, the consideration given is the payment of the policy premium, or the promise to pay the premium at a later date.

Image

Note: An insurance policy is valid even if the premium has not been paid, or fully paid, at the time of a claim, so long as the policyholder has an intent to pay. In practice, most brokerage offices request full payment or a payment plan once the client has offered to purchase a policy.

3.3.1.1.3 Legality of Object

Legality of object refers to whether the object of the contract has a legal basis. Contracts for an illegal purpose do not service the public good, so they are not enforceable by law. For example, a contract for importing illegal drugs is not enforceable.

From an insurance perspective, an insurance policy purchased on illegally acquired property would be unenforceable in the event of a loss to that property.

Image

3.3.1.1.4 Legal Capacity of the Parties to the Contract

For a contract to be considered legally enforceable, the parties involved must have the legal capacity to enter into a contract. The following are legal entities who can enter into contracts:

• An individual is a legal person who can enter into contracts, sue, or be sued.

• Businesses registered as sole proprietorships, partnerships, or corporations are also legal entities and have the same right to contract as individuals.

• If an unincorporated business uses a trade name for marketing purposes, the contract must be issued in the name of the individual(s) who own the business, and not the trade name.

Trade names are not always the legally registered name of a company. For example, Tony Costa does construction for a living. He decides to refer to this business as Tony Costa Construction. However, Tony has never registered his business as Tony Costa Construction — so when he files his taxes, it’s still as Tony Costa. In this case, “Tony Costa Construction” is a trade name.

However, if Tony decides to register his business name, then the words “Limited,” “Company,” or “Corporation” at the end of the name would indicate that the business is a legal entity. For example, Tony Costa Construction Limited, if listed on his taxes, would be a legal entity. When dealing with an individual or a small business, it’s important to distinguish the legal name (if any) from the trade name.

Image

3.3.1.1.4.1 Legally Incompetent Persons

The law says the parties to a contract must be competent and have the legal capacity to enter into a contract. If not, the person can walk away from the contract. The following are persons the law considers incompetent with regards to contracts:

• Persons who are mentally incapable: Any person who is incapable of understanding the full meaning and effect of any contract to which they have agreed to.

• Persons who are under the influence: Individuals under the influence of alcohol or drugs at the time a contract is entered into. However, the law makes it very difficult for such a person to get out of a legitimate contract on the excuse they were drunk or high.

• Minors: In Ontario, a minor is defined as being under the age of 18. However, minors do have the legal right to contract for necessities of life, such as food, clothing, and housing.

Image

Note: Sometimes minors own or rent property, and they will require insurance coverage. Brokers are permitted to sell insurance policies to minors, but they must make very sure the minor fully understands the terms of the insurance purchase, or the contract may be voided.

3.3.1.1.5 Genuine Intention

A contract is enforceable only when it can be shown the parties actually intended to enter into a contract. If the validity of a contract is questioned, it is necessary to show that the agreement between the parties was not affected by one or more of the following:

• Fraud (trickery).

• Duress (use or threat of force or illegal imprisonment).

• Concealment (misrepresentation of pertinent facts).

• Mistake (a legal document signed in error or by mistake).

Otherwise, the contract may be voided.

Image

3.3.1.2 Three Elements of Insurance Contracts

In addition to the five basic elements required of all enforceable contracts, insurance contracts must include the following three elements:

1) Insurable interest.

2) Utmost good faith.

3) Indemnity.

Let’s take a closer look at these critical insurance contract components.

3.3.1.2.1 Insurable Interest

Individuals and other legal entities have an insurable interest when they can show they would suffer financially from a loss. The following are examples of parties who can demonstrate an insurable interest:

• Owners of property.

• Mortgage holders (mortgagees).

• Bailees to whom property is entrusted for repair, service, or safekeeping (for example, dry cleaners, computer repair shops, garages, storage facilities).

• Any person or entity who may be legally liable to a third party for injury or damage. For example, since people are responsible for their negligent actions, they have an insurable interest in a liability insurance policy.

Note: Insurance law prevents people from collecting insurance on property they do not own or in which they otherwise have no financial interest. The idea is to stop insurance fraud and to prevent people from profiting from insurance.

Image

3.3.1.2.2 Utmost Good Faith

The complete honesty of the parties is critical to a legally enforceable insurance contract. Both insureds and insurers have obligations in complying with this requirement, as outlined below.

The insurance company:

• Must manage the pool of premiums paid by policyholders so there is sufficient money to pay valid claims.

• Must pay claims according to the terms of the policy and not try to “get out” of paying valid claims just to save money.

The insured:

• Must be truthful when making statements regarding the risk and details of previous claims, policy cancellations, and refusals of insurance. Insurers base their decisions to issue insurance policies on information contained in the applications.

• Must exercise honesty as to the extent of their loss, in the event that their property is lost or damaged. If a serious loss occurs, there is often no way to determine the value of the property lost or damaged in an insurance claim.

Image

3.3.1.2.3 Indemnity

The law restricts payments for claims under an insurance policy to the actual amount of financial loss suffered by the policyholder — no more and no less.

3.3.2 Void and Voidable Contracts

To be enforceable by law, an insurance contract must contain each of the eight elements listed earlier. If any one of these elements is missing or violated, the insurance contract may be void or voidable. But what do void and voidable mean?

• Void contract: A void contract of insurance is one which is considered never to have existed.

• Voidable contract: A voidable contract of insurance is one that may be rendered void or unenforceable for one or more of the reasons discussed earlier.

3.3.2.1 Breach of Contract

When a contract breaks down by action of one of the parties, there are remedies in law. We will see in the statutory conditions laid out in the Insurance Act that an action such as fraud would be cause for a breach of contract.

Black’s Law Dictionary defines breach of contract as “failure, without legal excuse, to perform any promise which forms the whole or part of a contract.”14 Such civil wrongs can be remedied by court action instituted by the injured party. Generally, a contract is seen as a private matter between the parties and only those parties, are permitted to take action for breach of contract.

In the genuine intent section, we learned that fraud runs counter to the concept of genuine intention. Fraud, when committed by an insured who had promised not to commit fraud, would constitute a breach of contract and the insurer could now take action.

When there is a breach or failure to fulfill contractual obligations, the courts may rule in the following manner:

• provide payment of damages to the injured party;

• enforce specific performance of the terms of the contract;

• grant an injunction prohibiting a party of the contract from performing certain acts, or ensuring a party to the contract performs certain acts; and

• permit rescission of the contract to return the parties substantially to their pre-contract positions.

Actions for breach of contract must be commenced within a certain time or else the right to sue will be taken away.

3.3.2.2 Negative Option Billing

We have just learned the structure of an insurance contract, and what it means to breach a contract. An insurance intermediary, either a broker or agent, could abuse the agreement and genuine intention through an illegal act known as negative option billing. Negative option billing is an unfair business practice where the intermediary adds goods or services onto a customer’s policy without gaining their consent. The negative means that the customer is forced to remove that coverage and face any penalties or fees in doing so (in other words, they are financially coerced to continue with it).

Let’s look at an example.

Joshua called a broker to move his auto insurance to another, cheaper insurer. The broker collects the information from the customer’s current policy and prepares the application to submit. The broker decides to increase the liability limits to $2M and increase the deductible on DCPD. The broker then submits and binds the coverage and returns to the customer the confirmation and pink slip. Joshua, upset, calls the broker to ask why these coverages were added, only to be told that to remove or reduce them would incur an administrative fee as the policy is now enforced.

Again, negative option billing is an illegal practice in Ontario that both RIBO and FSRA are actively monitoring across the financial industry.15

3.3.3 Binders

A binder is defined as a written or oral agreement giving temporary insurance protection to the insured, pending issue of the policy by the insurance company. As such, a binder is a temporary contract of insurance and is subject to the same terms and conditions applying to all contracts of insurance. For this reason, brokers must make sure all binders issued contain the complete details to be incorporated into the actual insurance contract.

Binders are typically valid for a period of 30 days, or when the actual policy is received by the client, whichever comes first. This period can be extended upon request if the insurance company is delayed in issuing the policy itself.

We’ll learn more about issuing insurance binders and broker binding authority in Chapter 4: The Insurance-Buying Process.

3.3.3.1 Oral Binders

A binder can be issued orally, but as a general rule, it should be confirmed immediately with a written binder. This practice avoids the potential for disagreement about terms, conditions, and coverage limits in the event a loss occurs before the policy itself has been issued.

Because insurance is fiduciary in nature, it is extremely important that the industry be regulated and that all contracts are legally enforceable to protect the insurance consumer. GLOSSARY

• Binder: A written or oral agreement from the insurer giving temporary protection, to insured pending issuance of the policy. Also known as “cover note” or “interim receipt”.

• Canadian Council of Insurance Regulators (CCIR): An association of Canadian insurance regulators working to strengthen regulatory oversight of the Canadian insurance industry.

• Effective date: The date an insurance policy comes into effect, or the date additional coverages come into force.

• Express contract: A legally binding agreement between two parties wherein the terms are clearly defined either orally or in writing.

• Fiduciary: Someone who has undertaken to act for and on behalf of another person in a particular matter, in circumstances that give rise to a relationship of trust and confidence.

• Financial Services Regulatory Authority of Ontario (FSRA): The Financial Services Regulatory Authority of Ontario is an independent regulatory agency created to improve consumer protection in regulated professions in Ontario, including insurance. FSRA is responsible for all automobile forms/underwriting approvals, rates, and claims forms.

• Implied contract: A contract that is not written or spoken, but which is assumed to exist based on the words and actions of the parties involved.

• Negative option billing: An illegal business practice where the intermediary adds goods or services onto a customer’s policy without gaining their consent. Also known as “negative option marketing”.

• Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI): An independent agency of the Government of Canada, established in 1987 to contribute to the safety and soundness of the Canadian financial system.

• Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act (PIPEDA): A federal act regulating how personal information is collected, used, and disclosed in Canada.

• Property and Casualty Insurance Compensation Corporation (PACICC): Industry-funded organization that protects eligible policyholders from undue financial loss in the event that a member insurer becomes insolvent. Membership is required for P&C insurers.

• Void contract: A contract of insurance legally considered never to have existed.

• Voidable contract: An insurance contract that may be rendered void or unenforceable for one or more reasons.
