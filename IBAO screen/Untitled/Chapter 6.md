6.1 INTRODUCTION

As we’ve learned, insurance is part of the financial industry. Because it deals with valuables (including money) belonging to others, those who advise on and sell insurance products to the general public need to be regulated. By that same token, brokers work between insurance companies and the public, so the funds that are agreed to between the public and the broker need to also be protected for the insurance company. Whether it is by offering policies or collecting premiums, brokers are intimately working with the valuables of others. This chapter takes a close look at the Registered Insurance Brokers of Ontario (RIBO) and their mandate as legislated in the Registered Insurance Brokers Act (RIB Act).

6.2 THE REGISTERED INSURANCE BROKERS ACT

The Registered Insurance Brokers Act (RIB Act) provides Ontario insurance brokers the authority to self-regulate their business and to protect the insurance consumer. The RIB Act Booklet, which comes with this textbook, is best thought of in two parts:

1) The Act and Regulations, which form the legislation.

2) The By-laws, which form a housekeeping section that provides the RIBO Council powers to make changes to administrative and domestic affairs of RIBO.

This chapter will walk you through the RIB Act, as well as its Regulations and By-laws, which you can find in the RIB Act Booklet.

Here’s a tip: follow along in the RIB Act Booklet by marking the each of the sections we cover with a post-it note for easy reference.

6.3 REGISTERED INSURANCE BROKERS OF ONTARIO

6.3.1 History and Role

Prior to October 1, 1981, brokers had been supervised by the Superintendent of Insurance, an employee of the provincial government. (Although the Superintendent of Insurance no longer supervises insurance brokers, they still oversee the Insurance Act of Ontario. They also directly oversee the direct writing insurance companies and insurance agents within the province. Since June 8, 2019, the Superintendent of Insurance has operated within the Financial Services Regulatory Authority of Ontario [FSRA].)

As of October 1, 1981, insurance brokers in Ontario were granted authority through provincial legislation to self-regulate under a new corporation named the Registered Insurance Brokers of Ontario, also known as RIBO or the Corporation. The mission of RIBO is to protect the public and regulate the insurance broker profession across Ontario. RIBO’s responsibilities include, but are not limited to, standards of integrity, self-discipline, and the establishment of the minimum standards and performance of all member brokers. All insurance brokers who operate in Ontario are under the direction of the RIBO Council.

Every year, RIBO submits an official year-end report to the Superintendent of Insurance. This report must be filed four months before the end of each financial year (the RIBO financial year ends July 31). The Superintendent of Insurance reviews the RIBO year-end report and examines it with the provincial Minister of Finance. The Superintendent of Insurance is also a member of the Corporation.

One important distinction to note is that insurance brokers in Ontario are responsible to their clients, whereas agents are responsible to their insurance company. Because brokers serve the public interest, it is especially important that the profession is self-regulated.

6.3.2 The RIBO Council

6.3.2.1 Role and Responsibilities

The RIBO Council is the governing body and board of directors of the Corporation. It therefore manages and administers the RIBO’s affairs. The RIBO Council may pass by-laws relating to the administrative and domestic affairs of the Corporation, consistent with the RIB Act and the Regulations.

The RIBO Council is responsible for forwarding copies of the by-laws and amendments to the Superintendent of Insurance and making copies available to the public at the RIBO office. The by-laws are used to make minor changes to the legislation.

6.3.2.2 Size and Composition

Under the RIB Act, the RIBO Council must be composed of eight elected members and three appointees (members of the public) who are appointed by the Lieutenant Governor in Council (LGC). However, the LGC can increase the size of the RIBO Council, as long as at least one-quarter of the Council consists of persons appointed by the LGC. For example, under Regulation 989: Composition of Council, the LGC increased the size of the RIBO Council to a total of 13 members, consisting of nine member brokers and four appointees.

The appointees are public members who are appointed by the LGC to bring public interest concerns to the RIBO Council. Each appointee will serve a term of four years. Appointees may run for reappointment to serve another four-year term, but cannot exceed a total of eight years on the RIBO Council.

The LGC may also appoint members of the public (who are not members of the Corporation or the RIBO Council) to a panel of lay persons. These appointees are eligible to serve as members of the Complaints Committees or Discipline Committee.

6.3.2.3 Election Process

Every year, on the first Monday of November, the RIBO Council will elect a president and one or more vice-presidents from among its members. Every individual member is permitted to vote in the election, but must be registered under the Act and not in default of the prescribed fee.

At every election, three persons are elected to the RIBO Council for a term of three years each. Members who (1) are in good standing, (2) are not the subject of professional misconduct, incompetence, or incapacity and (3) have not been revoked or suspended in the last six years are eligible to stand for election to the RIBO Council.

RIBO tries to provide a balance on the RIBO Council so all member brokerages are represented fairly. Therefore, candidates for election are from either (1) brokerages that employ 20 or more individual members or (2) brokerages that employ fewer than 20 individual members.

The Manager of RIBO must make all reasonable efforts to ensure that there are candidates for election to the RIBO Council from both categories.

6.3.3 RIBO Committees

The RIBO Council is responsible for establishing the following committees and appointing members to them:

• Qualification and Registration (Q&R) Committee.

• One or more Complaints Committees.

• Discipline Committee.

The RIBO Council may establish additional committees from time to time as it considers necessary. We’ll examine each committee, and its key responsibilities, in more detail below.

Image

6.3.3.1 Qualification and Registration Committee

The Qualification and Registration (Q&R) Committee oversees insurance broker candidacy. After candidates (who are qualified under the Regulations) have passed the examinations approved by the Council, the Manager refers these candidates to the Q&R Committee to issue certificates of registration and renewals.

Other responsibilities of the Q&R Committee include:

• Specifying additional examinations to applicants and members.

• Directing the Manager to issue or refuse certificates or renewals.

• Reviewing qualifications of members and imposing limitations on certificates pending demonstration of competence.

• Ensuring the Manager maintains one or more registers of all certificates issued, as well as all revocations, suspensions, cancellations, expirations, or other terminations.

If the Q&R Committee refuses to grant a certificate, the Manager will provide written reasons to the applicant on behalf of the committee. However, if the applicant’s refusal was previously registered and had been suspended or revoked based on a decision from the Discipline Committee, the Manager does not need to notify the applicant of the Q&R Committee’s reasons.

If the applicant submits a request for a hearing by mail or in person, the Q&R Committee must establish a hearing within 15 days of notice served. After a hearing or a review, the committee can do one of the following things:

• Confirm the proposed decision.

• Require the applicant to take qualifying exams, additional training, or both, as a condition.

• Direct the Manager to register the applicant, subject to conditions as the committee considers appropriate.

In inquiries involving potentially incapacitated members, the Q&R Committee also determines:

• Whether or not the member is incapacitated.

• If the member is incapacitated, whether to revoke, suspend, or attach terms and conditions to the member.

6.3.3.2 One or More Complaints Committees

The Complaints Committee will investigate complaints regarding any member of the Corporation for conduct or actions.

The committee investigates complaints only when:

• A written complaint has been filed with the Manager;

• The member has been notified of the complaint and given at least two weeks to submit, in writing to the committee, any explanation the member may wish to make concerning the matter; and

• The committee has examined all records and other documents relating to the complaint.

After reviewing the information it receives, the committee may:

• Direct the matter to be referred to the Discipline Committee, in whole or in part;

• Direct the matter not to be referred to the Discipline Committee, in whole or in part; or

• Take actions that it considers appropriate and that are not inconsistent with the RIB Act, Regulations, or By-laws.

Each Complaints Committee is composed of any number of people the RIBO Council determines necessary; however, at least one member of the Complaints Committee must be a person who is not a member of the Corporation and who has been appointed to the RIBO Council or panel of lay persons by the LGC. Additionally, the Council may appoint an individual member of the Corporation to a Complaints Committee. Finally, to keep the Complaints Committee unbiased, no member of the Discipline Committee can sit on this committee.

6.3.3.3 Discipline Committee

The RIBO Council or the Complaints Committee may direct the Discipline Committee to hold a hearing and determine any specific allegations of misconduct or incompetence on the part of a member. The Discipline Committee will also direct any allegations of mishandling of trust funds or filing of financial reports to the Manager, as defined in the Regulations.

When the Discipline Committee hears the allegations of misconduct or incompetence, it will:

• Consider the allegation hearing, review the evidence, and ascertain all the facts.

• Determine if the evidence and allegations have been proved.

• Determine if the member is guilty of misconduct or incompetence.

• Determine the penalty to be imposed on the member.

If a member is found unsuitable to carry on business as a registered insurance broker or found guilty of misconduct, as defined in Regulation 991, Section 15, the Discipline Committee may determine the following actions (or combination of actions):

• Revoke the certificate.

• Suspend the certificate.

• Reprimand the member.

• Impose a fine on the member, to be paid to the Treasurer of Ontario into the Consolidated Revenue Fund.

• Suspend or postpone for a period as determined by the committee.

• Reimburse the person who registered the complaint for costs.

When the committee revokes, suspends, or restricts the certificate of a member, the decision is effective immediately, even though there may be an appeal from the member. A copy of the decision will be served to the complainant.

If the committee determines that discipline was not warranted, it will reimburse the member for costs associated with the action.

The RIBO Council determines the size of the Discipline Committee; however, at least four members of the committee must be people who are not members of the Corporation but were appointed to the Council or to the panel of lay persons by the LGC. The RIBO Council may appoint any individual member of the Corporation to the Discipline Committee. Furthermore, the RIBO Council may appoint one of its members to be the Chair of the committee.

The Chair may assign a panel of five committee members to hold a hearing. One participant of the hearing must have been appointed by the LGC to the RIBO Council or to the panel of lay persons. The panel is considered sufficient to exercise the jurisdiction and powers of the committee if a quorum is present. If a member of the committee becomes incapable of continuing to act, the remaining members may complete the hearing.

6.3.3.3.1 Misconduct

Misconduct is referenced in Regulation 991, Section 15, which contains a set of guidelines of what would be considered poor behaviour for a broker. It’s not a simple definition; instead, it’s a set of guidelines that can be used both by the broker to understand how to behave, and by the Discipline Committee to justify a decision.

6.3.3.3.2 Incompetence

Incompetence is defined as when a member has, while acting as an insurance broker, displayed a serious lack of knowledge, skill, or judgment, or a serious disregard for the welfare of a member of the public.

6.3.4 Incapacitated Members

The RIB Act defines an “incapacitated member” as

“a member suffering from a physical or mental condition or disorder of a nature and extent making it desirable in the interest of the public or the member that he or she no longer be permitted to carry on business as a registered insurance broker or that his or her business be restricted.”

When the Manager receives information leading them to believe a member may be incapacitated, the Manager initiates an inquiry by reporting the information to the RIBO Council. The RIBO Council may then notify the member and appoint a board of inquiry (composed of at least two members of the Corporation and one member of the RIBO Council) to inquire into the matter.

The member may be required to submit to a mental or physical examination by a qualified person. If the member fails to submit to the examination, the board may order the member’s certificate suspended until the member complies. Following the inquiry, the Q&R Committee will determine:

• Whether or not the member is incapacitated.

• If the member is incapacitated, whether to revoke, suspend, or attach terms and conditions to the member.

6.3.5 Investigation of Members

When the Manager believes on reasonable and probable grounds that a member has committed an act of misconduct or incompetence, they may appoint one or more persons to open an investigation to determine if such act has actually occurred and report back to the Manager. If the member committed an act of misconduct or incompetence involving trust funds, then two individuals from the insurers for whom the funds were to be held in trust must be added to the investigation.

The member under investigation must allow the investigator to enter their premises at any reasonable time to examine the books and records of the brokerage, and is not allowed to obstruct the investigator. If the investigator is obstructed, they will attain a warrant to enter the premises with or without police, at any time between sunrise to sunset (unless a judge allows the premises to be searched at night).

6.3.6 Fines of Contravention of the RIB Act

If a member contravenes any provision of the RIB Act and is found guilty of an offence, they will be liable upon their conviction for a fine of $100,000, or imprisonment for a term up to six months, or both.

If the guilty member is a corporation (that is, a company or brokerage), then the maximum fine imposed is $200,000.

6.3.7 Publication of Findings

RIBO publishes its Bulletin every quarter, providing information about the Q&R and Discipline Committees. All findings for both committees are published and posted on the RIBO website for every member, and the public, to access. The Bulletin provides the decisions of the committees, unless it is determined that the information will adversely expose others. Prior to publication, RIBO provides a summary of the discipline to any member who has been listed in the Bulletin and informs them of the committee’s decision.

6.4 RIB ACT REGULATIONS

The Ontario Regulations are the working components of implementation of the RIB Act. The most important of these is Regulation 991, Section 14 (Code of Conduct) and Regulation 991, Section 15 (Misconduct).

6.4.1 Regulation 989: Composition of Council

We know from Section 6.3.2.2: Size and Composition that the LGC increased the size of the RIBO Council in the Act to include nine elected members and four appointees. Therefore, the current composition of the RIBO Council is a total of 13 members.

6.4.2 Regulation 990: Exemptions

Not everyone who works in a brokerage needs to be licensed as an insurance broker. (For example, administrative staff, such as receptionists, bookkeepers, and IT personnel would not require an insurance broker license.) Only employees who engage and advise the public on risks and sell insurance policies need to be licensed.

In addition to the administrative staff noted above, risk management consultants are also exempted from the licensing requirements. Although these professionals may speak about insurance in their discussions with their clients, they do not engage in the same type of activities as brokers. While their work may seem similar to what brokers do, risk management consultants have separate guidelines and expectations under another regulator (Risk Management Consultants of Ontario).

6.4.3 Regulation 991: General Regulations

6.4.3.1 Section 5: Who Can Be an Insurance Broker in Ontario?

To apply for an insurance broker license in Ontario, candidates must meet the following criteria:

• Must have reached the age of majority (18).

• Must be of good standing in the community.

• Have not been convicted of an offence.

• Have not been refused an insurance broker license, had one suspended, or had one revoked in the past.

• Have completed and passed (75% or higher) the RIBO Level 1 exam.

• Confirm that insurance broker will be the sole profession they will work in, as either a broker or life broker/agent

• Confirm that any other business practice they wish to pursue while working as an insurance broker has been pre-authorized by the Q&R Committee.

• Will meet and continue to meet the continuing education requirements established by the RIBO Council.

6.4.3.1.1 What is an Insurance Broker?

The RIB Act defines an “insurance broker” as:

“Any person who, for any compensation, commission or other thing of value, with respect to persons or property in Ontario, deals directly with the public and,

1) Acts or aids in any manner in soliciting, negotiating or procuring the making of any contract of insurance or reinsurance whether or not the person has agreements with insurers, allowing the person to bind coverage and countersign insurance documents on behalf of insurers,

2) Provides risk management services including claims assistance where required,

3) Provides consulting or advisory services with respect to insurance or reinsurance,

4) Holds himself, herself or itself out as an insurance consultant or examines, appraises, reviews or evaluates any insurance policy, plan or program or makes recommendations or gives advice with regard to any of the above.”

6.4.3.2 Section 6 to Section 7.2: What Are Insurance Brokerages?

The RIB Act outlines the types of businesses that insurance brokers can establish. These include.

• Corporation: A large organization with multiple layers of ownership and management, such as a board of directors.

• Partnership: Owned by two or more people, where the majority of equity interest and voting shares must be held by persons who are Registered Insurance Brokers.

• Sole proprietorship: A business owned by a single individual.

All insurance brokerages must designate an individual (who is also a licensed insurance broker) to be the principal broker of that business. The individual is qualified to be designated as the principal broker if they are not in default of any prescribed fees as established under the Act or Regulations, are not subject to any disciplinary proceedings, and have passed RIBO Level 2 exams.

The principal broker is often the owner or single manager of the brokerage. They are ultimately responsible for directing and supervising the brokerage, and ensuring the brokerage and all its staff adhere to the Regulations. The RIB Act Booklet outlines eight key responsibilities for principal brokers.

6.4.3.3 Section 8: Conflicts of Interest Preventing Issuance of Certificates of Registration

The Q&R Committee will not issue a certificate of registration to anyone whose office space, by virtue of being located on the same premises, is in a position to offer inducement, or use coercion or undue influence in order to control, direct, or secure insurance business. This Regulation makes it clear that lending institutions, such as banks or credit unions, cannot provide insurance products in the same office location. For example, if a bank issues a car loan and sells insurance products in the same location, the bank staff could have intimate knowledge of the customer and could then be incentivized to add automobile insurance into the sale of the loan. To protect the public from potential conflicts of interest, these two entities must be separated both physically (that is, separate offices/locations) and through business functions (that is, a loan officer cannot be a licensed insurance agent).

6.4.3.4 Section 9: Certificates of Insurance

When members of the public place insurance with one or more insurers, they must be provided with a certificate of insurance within 21 days after the insurance has been secured or confirmed.

The certificate of insurance must contain the following:

• The name and mailing address of the insured.

• A description of the coverage provided.

• The full name of each insurer, or other person authorized to undertake the contract.

• The amount of insurance placed with each insurer.

6.4.3.5 Section 10: Using Unlicensed Insurers

Most insurance policies are placed with federally or provincially licensed insurers. All insurance companies in Ontario are regulated by the Superintendent of Insurance. However, some exposures are too great a risk for insurance companies to protect. In these circumstances, one might look to place these insurance policies with unlicensed insurers.

This is not a common process, but it does exist as a legitimate possibility for insurance brokers facing a difficult risk to insure. When placing insurance with unlicensed insurers, the following requirements apply:

• The broker has informed the member of the public that:

- The insurer is not licensed in Ontario.

- The insurer is not subject to regulations under the Insurance Act.

- The orderly payment of claims may be more difficult when the insurer is not under regulation of the Insurance Act.

- The Superintendent of Insurance has no authority over this insurer or its policies.

- The broker must submit provincial and federal taxes.

• The broker must obtain written permission from the insured before the policy is placed.

• If the risk is automobile coverage, the minimum amount of liability ($200,000) must be placed with a licensed provincial insurer.

• The broker must inform the Superintendent of Insurance within 30 days of each quarter of all policies placed with unlicensed insurers.

6.4.3.6 Section 12: Fees Charged in Addition to Commissions Received

In some cases, brokers will charge a fee to a client in addition to the commission received under the policy. This is a common practice and occurs when the servicing of a policy requires extra administration activities to properly maintain a client’s file (such as a business’s commercial fleet policy).

In these circumstances, the broker must advise the policyholder prior to charging the fee, and it must be clear that this fee is in addition to the premium being charged. The broker must clearly declare to the client:

• The amount of the fee being charged.

• The portion of the retained premium.

• The total remuneration on the transaction.

6.4.3.7 Section 14: Code of Conduct

All professionals who are regulated under the RIB Act are also expected to uphold the insurance profession in a fiduciary capacity. Regulation 991, Section 14 describes the proper behaviours members must adhere to at all times when acting as an insurance broker.

The following list summarizes the 15 items constituting good behaviour:

1) Discharging your duties to the public with integrity.

2) Owing a duty to be competent when performing on behalf of clients and prospective clients.

3) Being conscientious, diligent, and efficient, and providing a quality of service equal to other members.

4) Being both candid and honest.

5) Holding information of clients in strict confidence (unless negotiating with underwriters).

6) Following all rules and laws in safekeeping information and property of clients.

7) Conducting business so that any other business associations will not jeopardize the member’s role as an insurance broker.

a) Disclosing in writing, in advance, any financial relationship with insurers.

8) Fully disclosing any fee charged and ensuring that such fees are proportionate for the services provided.

9) Encouraging public respect.

10) Making services available in an efficient and convenient manner.

11) Assisting in maintaining the integrity of the vocation.

12) Assisting in preventing others who are not authorized to sell insurance as members.

13) Acting with courtesy and good faith.

14) Co-operating in any investigation conducted by the RIBO.

15) Notifying RIBO of incompetence and misconduct in any other jurisdiction.

6.4.3.8 Section 15: Misconduct

Regulation 991, Section 15 outlines the actions that brokers should never take. The following list summarizes the 16 items constituting bad behaviour:

1) Using methods of soliciting with incomplete and/or false statements of the insurance contract.

2) Using incomplete comparisons to induce a prospective client to agree to an insurance contract.

3) Using any payment, allowance, or gift as an inducement for prospective clients to change brokers.

4) Rebating any portion of the premium to be paid.

5) Using a professional or business relationship to coerce or induce to control a prospective client.

6) Advertising under a different member’s name or brokerage than what is registered through RIBO.

7) Causing any unreasonable delay or resistance of fair payment of claims.

8) Failing to carry on business consistent with the Code of Conduct.

9) Failing to comply with the Act, Regulations, or By-laws of RIBO.

10) Acting as an insurance agent.

11) Being convicted after October 1, 1981 of a criminal offence under the Insurance Act.

12) Charging or paying a referral fee or finder’s fee, other than with occupations currently allowed under the Regulations.

13) Serving as a director, officer, or principal broker and concurring with misconduct of a member of a sole proprietorship, partnership, or corporation.

14) Providing false or misleading information to RIBO.

15) Acting as a principal broker when failing to comply with the educational requirements.

16) Serving as a principal broker and failing to report that a member broker of the brokerage has committed misconduct.

6.4.3.9 Section 15.1: Findings of Incompetence or Misconduct

Findings of incompetence, misconduct, or similar against a member or insurance agent in any jurisdiction outside of Ontario must be reported to RIBO.

6.4.3.10 Section 16: Trust Funds and Trust Accounts

All premiums received by members are called trust funds. These amounts must be deposited into the brokerage’s named trust account and designated as a trust fund. The transfer must be made no later than three banking days after the day the trust money is received. These trust funds are payable to the insurance companies under a specified time period, with conditions defined in the contract with the insurance company.

The premiums received by the brokerage also include the commissions that will be made payable to the brokerage. These commissions must be transferred from the trust account directly into the general account at a separate time.

The general account is a business bank account that is held under the brokerage’s name, with the same financial institution as the trust account. The financial institution must be approved by RIBO Council.

6.4.3.11 Section 19 and Section 20: Errors and Omissions Insurance and Fidelity Insurance

Every brokerage must purchase errors and omissions (E&O) protection for professional mistakes and fidelity protection for dishonest acts of brokerage employees. There is always a deductible associated with E&O policies. Fidelity policies will usually have a deductible to lower premiums; alternatively, the underwriter may include one, especially if there have been past claims.

Equity capitalization is calculated as the total assets less total liabilities. Every broker must maintain, at all times, the maximum amount of the deductible of these policies. If the broker is a sole proprietor, the equity capitalization must be no less than $2,500. If the brokerage is a corporation or partnership, then the equity capitalization must be no less than $5,000.

Each brokerage must maintain a minimum $3,000,000 limit on their E&O policy and $100,000 on their fidelity policy; however, many brokerages carry much higher limits.

6.4.3.12 Section 21: Form 1 Position Report

The Form 1 Position Report is a financial report that identifies a brokerage’s financial year-end trust account, general account, and equity total. This report is signed by the principal broker and must be completed and received by RIBO within 90 days of the brokerage’s fiscal year-end. A second Form 1 Position Report must be filed six months after the brokerage’s fiscal year-end, within 90 days.

6.4.3.13 Section 22: Fines for Misconduct

When a brokerage is found guilty of misconduct, the maximum fine against the brokerage is $100,000. When an individual member is found guilty of misconduct, the maximum amount is $25,000.

6.4.4 By-laws

In March 2024, RIBO replaced approximately 30 By-laws that had been on their mandate for 40 years with three consolidated and updated new ones. As of March 2024, these By-laws are in effect and tested in your Level 1 exam. In the following sections, we have identified some of the key items within these By-laws, but you can find more information in your RIB Act Booklet.

6.4.4.1 By-law No. 1, Article 6: The Council

This By-law establishes who is eligible for election to the RIBO Council and, as mentioned earlier in Section 6.3.2.2: Size and Composition, it also establishes the two electoral categories of candidates for election. Brokerages can range in size from small to very large, and this By-law helps to bring accurate demographic representation into the election. Candidates for election can represent either (1) brokerages of 20 or more members or (2) candidates from brokerages with fewer than 20 members.

The Manager of RIBO will make all reasonable efforts to ensure that at least one candidate for election is from each category.

This is also the section where RIBO has adjusted the number of people on Council to be up from what is stated in Regulation 989. This By-law states there will be 13 people on Council, including 9 members and 4 appointees.

6.4.4.2 By-law No. 3, Article 2: Registration and Related Matters

Membership in RIBO is maintained on a yearly basis. The term period is between October 1 to September 30 each year.

The renewal application, along with all renewal fees, must be filed with RIBO no later than August 31 each year. If the application and fees are not filed in time, the broker’s membership will be suspended as of September 30 of that year. The member has one year to renew their membership. If the renewal is not received within one year by RIBO, the certificate will expire.

Additionally, any information about the broker that has changed — such as their brokerage, legal name, or address — must be updated with RIBO within 10 days of the change.

6.4.4.3 By-law No. 3, Article 3: Individual Classes of Registration

Some brokerages specialize in certain types of insurance classes. The list below describes what the RIBO Level 1, Level 2, and Level 3 licence holders are held to, but it is important to know that the Level 1 licence requires the holder to work for two years under supervision (similar to an apprenticeship) before they can qualify to attempt their RIBO Level 2 or Level 3 licence. It also means that owning a brokerage is not as straightforward as just starting one or buying one; there are specific rules to brokerage ownership that RIBO has mandated in the Act. Additionally, many brokerages are licensed in more than one province in Canada. RIBO has established different classes of membership for these situations.

Each class of membership has different terms and conditions. Most brokerages in Ontario provide insurance for all classes of business, other than life and health insurance (these are regulated provincially, but not by RIBO).

RIBO identifies the following classes of membership:

• Level 1 licensee, who acts under the supervision of a Principal Broker or Level 3 licensee.

• Level 2 licensee, who acts under the supervision of a Principal Broker or Level 3 licensee.

• Level 3 licensee, who has been either a Level 1 or Level 2 licensee for 24 months or longer, but is only a Principal Broker once appointed under Article 6.

6.4.4.4 By-law No. 3, Article 4: Firm Classes of Registration

No two brokerages are exactly the same, and there are different forms a brokerage business can take. In this article, RIBO spells out the types of firms that can register themselves as a brokerage. They include:

• Active firm licensee.

• Non-active firm licensee.

• Consultant firm licensee.

6.4.4.5 By-law No. 3, Article 5: Out-of-Province Licensees

Many brokerages do business in other provinces — sometimes as part of their business growth strategy — other times because they have clients who live or work in other provinces, so RIBO created new licence categories:

• Out-of-province licensee.

• Out-of-Canada licensee.

All RIBO licensed Brokers are required to complete a fixed number of accredited continuing education courses to renew their membership yearly. Based on the license, Level 1 or Level 3, there is a set requirement of hours per category to complete.

Ethics 	Min. 1 Hr 	Min. 1 Hr
Technical 	Min. 3 Hr 	Optional
Professional Development 	Max. 2 Hr 	N/A
Management 	0 - Max. 4 Hr 	Min. 5 Hr

However, a newly licensed Level 1 Broker is exempt from this requirement within their first year. Upon their first renewal on October 1st the minimum number of hours per category is enforced.

6.5 CONCLUSION

This concludes our overview of the regulation of insurance brokers in Ontario. We’ve covered the key points of the RIB Act and the RIB Act Booklet that came with this textbook. Although the RIB Act, Regulations, and By-laws may seem highly prescriptive, it’s important to remember that these rules and guidelines are in place to protect the general public and to uphold the integrity of the insurance broker profession. They’re vital for you to learn not only for your RIBO Level 1 exam, but also to become a competent and compliant broker. See you in Chapter 7!

GLOSSARY

• Code of conduct: As defined under Regulation 991, Section 14.

• Corporation: A business operation that declares the business as a separate, legal entity guided by a group of officers known as the board of directors.

• Direct writers: An insurance company that sells its policies directly to customers without using intermediaries.

• Discipline Committee: A discipline panel comprising four brokers and a public member that conducts hearings very similar to those of a court of law. Evidence is introduced during the hearing, and testimony is given under oath in the presence of a court reporter.

• Fidelity: Employee theft of property of others including, but not limited to, premiums, employer’s client information, and tangible property (such as stock or equipment).

• General account: An account where the brokerage must deposit the commissions from its trust account.

• Incapacitated member: A member suffering from a physical or mental condition or disorder who is unable to perform duties for the welfare of the public interest.

• Incompetence: Displaying a serious lack of knowledge, skill, or judgment, or a serious disregard for the welfare of a member of the public.

• Insurance Act: Provincial legislation that regulates and controls insurance through Queen’s Park.

• Investigator: An individual authorized by RIBO to investigate consumer complaints and is responsible for conducting checks of brokerage compliance to RIBO regulations.

• Lieutenant Governor in Council (LGC): As the Crown’s representative in Ontario, the Lieutenant Governor in Counsel exercises constitutional powers and promotes a strong and positive civic community.

• Manager: CEO of Registered Insurance Brokers of Ontario (RIBO).

• Members: A person registered under the Registered Insurance Brokers Act to carry on business as a Registered Insurance Broker.

• Misconduct: As defined under Regulation 991, Section 15.

• Panel of lay persons: Public members, who are not on the RIBO Council, appointed by the LGC to serve on the Complaints Committees or Discipline Committee.

• Partnership: Two or more persons in a partnership, where the majority of equity and voting interest is held by brokers.

• Principal broker: A partner of the partnership, or a full-time employee and/or sole proprietor of the sole proprietorship, with authority to act in the name of, and on behalf of, the firm regarding information filed to comply with all RIBO requirements.

• Public members: Individual members who are appointed to the RIBO Council or the panel of lay persons by the LGC.

• Qualification and Registration (Q&R) Committee: Determines the eligibility of individuals and firms, and sets competency standards for insurance brokers and brokerages in Ontario.

• RIBO Council: The governing body and board of directors of the Corporation.

• Self-regulation: The act of self-governing and self-supporting general insurance brokers in Ontario.

• Sole proprietor: Sole proprietorship is not a legal entity. It simply refers to a person who owns the business and is personally responsible for its debts.

• Superintendent of Insurance: An individual who is appointed to oversee all insurance in the province of Ontario.

• Trust funds: Trust funds comprise (1) all funds received from a client in payment of insurance premiums for payment to an insurer and (2) all funds received from an insurer for refund to a client.

• Unlicensed Insurers: Property and casualty insurance companies that are not regulated by the Superintendent of Insurance in the province.
